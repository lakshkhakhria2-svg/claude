# Klara's Sun turns "superstition" back on science

Ishiguro presents the Sun as a god without a religion. Klara is a solar-powered Artificial Friend who reasons her way into worshipping it: she gives it a capital letter and a male pronoun, prays to it, and bargains with it for Josie's life. Meanwhile the humans around her trust gene editing ("lifting") and Mr Capaldi's plan to "continue" Josie through AI. Ishiguro has called her devotion **"kind of a religious thing"** and said he wanted to write about "that instinct without any of the baggage of organized religion". He has also said he was **not "trying to be critical of religion"** ([A Word on Words](https://awordonwords.org/klara-and-the-sun-kazuo-ishiguro/); [NPR Fresh Air](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/978138547)). The novel neither mocks Klara's faith nor confirms it. Its sharpest move is that the charge of superstition changes target, and that makes a natural spine for a 50-minute essay. In Part Four the Father fears that belief in human uniqueness is **"A kind of superstition we kept going while we didn't know better"**. In Part Six Rick, who once dismissed Klara's devotion as **"AF superstition"**, admits he keeps "wondering if there was more to it". Context gives that reversal its edge. The novel came out in March 2021, five months after CRISPR gene editing won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. It also came out in the year of the census that first recorded **fewer than half of people in England and Wales (46.2%) as Christian** ([Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2020/press-release/); [ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/culturalidentity/religion/bulletins/religionenglandandwales/census2021)). Against that background, a machine praying to the Sun reads as the oldest religious instinct coming back inside the newest technology. Critics divide along the same line. At one end is faith **"finally, rewarded"** (David Hoekema); at the other is a Sun whose answer to prayer is **"dubious"** (Rachel Griffis). That disagreement is itself evidence that Ishiguro left the question open. The material falls into three body paragraphs: a god Klara builds from evidence, a Sun set against a world that trusts science, and an ending that withholds a verdict. One caution applies to all of it. No interview or review page could be opened during the research, so only the novel quotations are fully confirmed. Everything else should be quoted in short fragments or paraphrased.

## A solar-powered machine reinvents the oldest religion

Ishiguro gives Klara every outward sign of devotion and removes every sign of an actual religion. She writes the Sun with a capital letter and calls it "he". A Skidmore College essay, "Klara's Religion", treats those two details as evidence that she sees it as a conscious being ([Skidmore](https://www.skidmore.edu/fye/summer_reading/2023/essays-and-reflection/posts/klaras-religion.php)). She trusts in its "special nourishment" and "kindness" ([NYRB](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2021/11/04/kazuo-ishiguro-klara-robot-woman/)). In *Plough*'s words, she "speaks about the sun as a person of faith would speak about God: with reverence, awe, petition" ([Plough](https://www.plough.com/en/topics/culture/literature/klara-and-the-sun-review)). Ishiguro roots this faith in her body. Because she runs on sunlight, he explained, "she thinks everything good comes from the sun. That's where she gets her nourishment". Looking out of the shop window, she assumes the people in the street are "in the same position" ([A Word on Words](https://awordonwords.org/klara-and-the-sun-kazuo-ishiguro/)). Her proof is something she sees while still in the store: the Beggar Man and his dog seem to have died in a doorway, then revive in the morning sun. **Klara reaches her religion by the method of science.** She observes, then generalises from limited evidence, so the line between faith and science blurs from the opening pages. Ishiguro has described her at the start as "like a tabula rasa" (a blank slate), "quite childlike and very open" ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2022/04/08/1091256917/kazuo-ishiguro-draws-on-his-songwriting-past-to-write-novels-about-the-future); [TIME](https://time.com/5943376/kazuo-ishiguro-interview/)). The book began as an idea for a picture book, which his daughter Naomi warned would "traumatize" young children ([The Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/arts/books/article-british-author-kazuo-ishiguro-returns-with-klara-and-the-sun-his-first/)). That origin helps explain the fairy-tale logic of Klara's later bargain with the Sun.

Asked what Klara's relationship with the Sun is, Ishiguro called it **"kind of a religious thing"**. He said he wanted to write about "that instinct without any of the baggage of organized religion", meaning the centuries of power, politics, tribalism and war that have built up around religion ([A Word on Words](https://awordonwords.org/klara-and-the-sun-kazuo-ishiguro/)). That aim explains an absence the *Church Times* reviewer Catherine Pickford pointed out: "God is not mentioned once" ([Church Times](https://www.churchtimes.co.uk/articles/2021/30-july/books-arts/book-reviews/klara-and-the-sun-by-kazuo-ishiguro)). The Sun is faith stripped down to its instinct, which is dependence, gratitude and hope, with the doctrine taken out. The Booker Prizes' official reading guide invites readers to compare Klara's belief with human religious faith ([Booker Prizes](https://thebookerprizes.com/the-booker-library/features/reading-guide-klara-and-the-sun-by-kazuo-ishiguro)). Ishiguro's own background fits this loose, non-doctrinal view. On *Fresh Air* he described his Japanese family as nominally Zen Buddhist but not practising ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2021/03/17/978138547/kazuo-ishiguro-draws-on-his-songwriting-past-to-write-novels-about-the-future)). In the real world, robots and religion had already met: since 2019 a 400-year-old Buddhist temple in Kyoto has used an android called Mindar to preach sermons ([SCMP](https://www.scmp.com/news/asia/east-asia/article/3022716/meet-mindar-humanoid-robot-preaches-sermons-buddhist-temple)). Ishiguro reverses the direction. His robot does not preach to humans; it prays.

Measured against the history of religion, Klara's god is the oldest one available. Egypt worshipped Re as sun and creator. The pharaoh Akhenaton made the Aton, a sun disc whose rays end in human hands, the only god ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/sun-worship); [Britannica: Aton](https://www.britannica.com/EBchecked/topic/41851/Aton)). Greece linked Apollo with both the sun and healing ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/summary/Apollo-Greek-mythology)). Japan puts the sun goddess Amaterasu at the heart of its mythology ([Nippon.com](https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-topics/g00748/amaterasu-the-japanese-sun-goddess.html)). Ishiguro was born there, in Nagasaki, in 1954, and his family moved to Guildford when he was five ([Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/literature/2017/ishiguro/facts/)). In the Bible, Malachi promises that "the Sun of righteousness" will "arise with healing in his wings" ([Bible Gateway](https://www.biblegateway.com/verse/en/Malachi%204:2)). That one verse holds all three features of Klara's Sun: a capital "S", a male pronoun and the power to heal. According to Izaak Walton, John Donne wrote "A Hymn to God the Father" during a dangerous illness in 1623. In it he begs that "thy Son / Shall shine as he shines now", punning on "Son" and "sun" ([Poetry Foundation](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/44115/a-hymn-to-god-the-father)). A sick man pleading with a "he" who shines is close to Klara pleading for the sick Josie. Klara arrives at this tradition on her own, at the moment her makers' culture is finishing its retreat from it. In 1882 Nietzsche's madman announces that "God is dead" and pictures humanity drifting "Away from all suns" ([Fordham Sourcebook](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/mod/nietzsche-madman.asp)). In the **2021 Census of England and Wales, 46.2% described themselves as Christian, down from 59.3% in 2011, and 37.2% reported no religion** ([ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/culturalidentity/religion/bulletins/religionenglandandwales/census2021); [Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/world/uk/2022/11/29/england-and-wales-are-minority-christian-countries)). In the United States the Christian share had fallen to 63% and the share with no religion had risen to 29% ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2021/12/14/about-three-in-ten-u-s-adults-are-now-religiously-unaffiliated/)). World Values Survey data found that 43 of 49 countries became less religious between 2007 and 2019 ([World Values Survey](https://www.worldvaluessurvey.org/WVSNewsShow.jsp?ID=421)).

Critics hear this ancient note. Judith Shulevitz writes in *The Atlantic* that Ishiguro "drapes realism like a thin cloth over a primordial cosmos". When the cloth slips, it reveals "the old gods" ([Penguin Random House](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/653825/klara-and-the-sun-by-kazuo-ishiguro/)). *The Spectator*'s reviewer sees the Sun turning, for Klara, into "a sort of primitive god" ([The Spectator](https://www.spectator.co.uk/?p=78025)). The Catholic journal *Dappled Things* draws the opposite conclusion from the same evidence: Klara "may be a manufactured android, but in Ishiguro's telling she is, quite remarkably, a believer" ([Dappled Things](https://www.dappledthings.org/reviews/the-faith-of-androids-kazuo-ishiguros-klara-and-the-sun)). The strongest claim for the first body paragraph is this: the Sun lets Ishiguro dramatise the instinct for faith inside a culture that thinks it has outgrown faith. The capital "S" and the pronoun "he" raise a star into a person. The reader recognises the shape of prayer even though no religion is ever named.

## The lifted world trusts science, yet Capaldi still asks for "faith"

A *Front Porch Republic* essay sums up the novel's world in two sentences: "the humans believe in science. The titular character, however, believes in the Sun" ([Front Porch Republic](https://frontporchrepublic.com/?p=44975)). Ishiguro had sketched that world in his **2017 Nobel Lecture**. There he warned: "New genetic technologies – such as the gene-editing technique CRISPR – and advances in Artificial Intelligence and robotics will bring us amazing, life-saving benefits, but may also create savage meritocracies that resemble apartheid, and massive unemployment" ([Nobel Lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/literature/2017/ishiguro/lecture/); [The Week](https://theweek.com/articles/978190/kazuo-ishiguros-klara-illuminates-what-meritocracy-does-soul)). The sentence reads like a blueprint for the novel: lifted and unlifted children, Klara herself, and the Father "substituted" at work. *Klara and the Sun* was published on 2 March 2021, his first novel since the Nobel ([The Bookseller](https://thebookseller.com/news/new-ishiguro-novel-coming-march-2021-1206788); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klara_and_the_Sun)). A *Guardian* interview ran just before publication under a headline in his own words: **"AI, gene-editing, big data … I worry we are not in control of these things any more"** ([The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2021/feb/20/kazuo-ishiguro-klara-and-the-sun-interview)). On CBC Radio he also said he expects these technologies to bring enormous benefits, and that people are not engaging with them enough ([CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/radio/thecurrent/the-current-for-march-1-2021-1.5931671/nobel-author-kazuo-ishiguro-on-artificial-intelligence-and-what-it-means-to-be-human-1.5932385)). His attitude is mixed feelings, not fear of technology. The Sun is the novel's counterweight: **the one power in the book that nobody designs, edits, copies or controls**.

The real science behind "lifting" made headlines in the years before publication. Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna won the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, announced on 7 October 2020, for CRISPR, which the Royal Swedish Academy called "a tool for rewriting the code of life" ([Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2020/press-release/); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2020/10/07/921043046/2-female-scientists-awarded-nobel-prize-in-chemistry-for-genome-editing-research)). **In late November 2018 He Jiankui announced the birth of the first gene-edited babies**, twin girls. A Chinese court sentenced him to three years in prison in December 2019 ([Boston University](https://www.bu.edu/sph/2018/12/04/how-did-claims-of-crispr-babies-hijack-an-international-gene-editing-summit); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2019/12/30/792456775/chinese-scientist-he-jiankui-sentenced-to-3-years-in-prison-for-editing-human-ge)). Ishiguro explained on *Fresh Air* that "lifted" is the novel's everyday word for children who have been gene-edited ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-6022105)). The novel implies that Josie's illness is a cost of the lifting her mother chose; check the passage in your copy before stating it as fact. In "The Case Against Perfection", the philosopher Michael Sandel argues that genetic enhancement comes from a drive to "mastery" that wears away our sense of life as a gift ([Sandel](https://sandel.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/case-against-perfection-whats-wrong-designer-children-bionic-athletes-and)). Klara stands on the other side of that line. She receives the Sun's help as a gift and answers it with gratitude and sacrifice. Mr Capaldi pushes mastery furthest. Thomas Jones argues in the *London Review of Books* that the novel's real question is less whether a mind can be built than whether a person can be copied ([LRB](https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v43/n06/thomas-jones/oh-you-darling-robot)). That question had a real-world parallel. The chatbot Replika began as its founder's attempt to recreate a dead friend from his text messages, and about half a million people downloaded it in April 2020 alone ([The Batch](https://www.deeplearning.ai/the-batch/bots-dont-need-social-distancing); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replika)).

Klara's faith turns this technological world into a moral landscape of light and dark. Her bargain is to destroy the Cootings Machine, whose Pollution she sees as the Sun's enemy, in return for the Sun's help for Josie. In a 2025 article in *Culture as Text*, Jack Robinson reads the deal, and Klara's wishful thinking around it, as her attempt to influence the Sun ([De Gruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/cat-2025-0004/html)). Hoekema reads the Sun's struggle with Pollution as a Manichaean battle, meaning a war between light and darkness ([The Christian Century](https://www.christiancentury.org/books/robot-learns-be-child)). Shulevitz's phrase "warring forces of light and darkness" makes the same point ([Penguin Random House](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/653825/klara-and-the-sun-by-kazuo-ishiguro/)). Klara carries out the bargain at a cost to her own body. The Catholic site *Word on Fire* calls it a "magnificent irony" that a product of technology sees beyond technology because of her faith. It also notes that her faith draws others into her mission, including Josie's father ([Word on Fire](https://www.wordonfire.org/articles/a-ratzingerian-reading-of-ishiguros-klara-and-the-sun/)). The pattern reverses *Frankenstein* (1818) ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Frankenstein-or-The-Modern-Prometheus)). Mary Shelley's created being turns on its maker. Ishiguro's is the most devoted character in the book, and the overreaching belongs to the humans.

The strongest evidence for this paragraph comes from the scientists' own mouths. In Part Four Mr Capaldi tells Chrissie "You have to keep faith, Chrissie" and "You mustn't lose faith". Later he insists **"It's not faith you need. Only rationality."** The man of science reaches for the language of faith before rejecting it, which suggests his project depends on belief just as Klara's does. Ruma Khatun reads the later line as Capaldi's cold, clinical rejection of faith ([Literariness Journal](https://literarinessjournal.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Solar-Faith-and-Synthetic-Hearts-Analog-Desire-in-Ishiguros-Digital-Future-in-Klara-and-the-Sun-Ruma-Khatun.pdf)). The Father then turns the word "superstition" on humanity itself. He asks Klara whether she believes in "the human heart", meant "in the poetic sense", as one review glosses it ([Mind Matters](https://mindmatters.ai/2023/02/klara-and-the-sun-a-review/)). He then confesses he fears Capaldi is right that science can copy and transfer whatever is inside a person. If so, belief in human uniqueness was **"A kind of superstition we kept going while we didn't know better"** ([context of the passage: Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/84460796-klara-and-the-sun?page=3)). The Father has lost his job to machines, and he now applies to the human heart the same reasoning that Nietzsche's century applied to God. He also revives a debate Alan Turing joined in 1950, when he rejected the "Theological Objection" that only a soul-bearing human can think ([Turing, 1950](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall13/cos402/readings/turing.pdf)). Ishiguro has said the AI debate made him think about two kinds of truth: scientific truth, and what people feel to be true in their hearts ([Science Friday](https://www.sciencefriday.com/?p=84963)). The second body paragraph can end on exactly that tension. In a world that trusts measurement, both Klara's Sun and the Father's "heart" get dismissed as superstition. Meanwhile Capaldi's own appeals to "faith" undercut his claim to pure "rationality".

## Josie recovers, Rick wonders, and Ishiguro withholds the verdict

The ending hands the question back to the reader. Josie recovers after the Sun pours into her room, but every word of that account is Klara's. No doctor, parent or narrator confirms her explanation. In Part Six Rick looks back: **"At the time, I thought it was all, well, AF superstition. Something just to bring us good luck. But these days, I keep wondering if there was more to it."** The word "superstition" moves from certainty into doubt. Rick took the sceptic's view at the time, and he ends up where Ishiguro leaves the reader. Ishiguro's comments keep the question open without settling it. When Terry Gross suggested on *Fresh Air* that the novel might be a critique of religion, he replied, "I don't think I was trying to be critical of religion". He added that Klara would "reflect that aspect of humanity, like she reflects other aspects of humanity" ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/978138547)). No interview found has him saying whether the Sun really healed Josie. *Bookforum*'s reviewer concludes that he "leaves open the question of whether Klara's ardent prayer, her deal with her Sun God, succeeds" ([Bookforum](https://www.bookforum.com/print/2801/kazuo-ishiguro-looks-at-the-world-through-a-robot-s-eyes-24397)). The Swedish Academy's Nobel citation praised Ishiguro for uncovering "the abyss beneath our illusory sense of connection with the world" ([Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/literature/2017/ishiguro/facts/)). Klara's bond with the Sun sits right on the edge of that abyss. It could be exactly such an illusion or something real, and the novel refuses to choose.

Critics divide three ways, and the split follows their starting beliefs. Reviewers in religious publications read the faith as real. Hoekema writes that "like Job and Elijah, Klara proposes a bargain" and that "Klara's faith in the Sun is, finally, rewarded" ([The Christian Century](https://www.christiancentury.org/books/robot-learns-be-child)). Pickford calls the book "a story about the power of faith" ([Church Times](https://www.churchtimes.co.uk/articles/2021/30-july/books-arts/book-reviews/klara-and-the-sun-by-kazuo-ishiguro)). Secular critics treat the Sun as something to study from outside, like an anthropologist, or even as horror. James Wood describes Ishiguro's fiction as "godless allegories" ([Penguin Random House](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/653825/klara-and-the-sun-a-gma-book-club-pick-by-kazuo-ishiguro/9780593311295/)). Mieko Anders in *Literary Hub* reads the novel's sun-worship as daylight folk horror alongside the film *Midsommar* ([Literary Hub](https://lithub.com/kazuo-ishiguros-daytime-horror-on-cults-white-supremacy-and-pagan-aesthetics)). Between them are readers who keep the question open. Griffis grants that "The Sun does sustain Josie, as well as Klara and all other forms of life" but judges that "Whether the Sun answers her prayers, though, is dubious" ([Front Porch Republic](https://www.frontporchrepublic.com/2022/01/ishiguros-new-novel-contemplates-the-relationship-between-humans-machines-and-the-natural-world/)). NPR's Maureen Corrigan argues that "Klara's misperception of the Sun as a caring deity calls to question our own limited human understanding" ([NPR review via TPR](https://www.tpr.org/2021-03-03/klara-and-the-sun-is-a-masterpiece-about-life-love-and-mortality)). *Reactor*'s reviewer admits that readers "begin by finding Klara's quasi-religious faith in this quest to be ridiculous, even sad". The review then asks whether it is any more absurd than our own weak efforts to protect future generations from climate disaster ([Reactor](https://reactormag.com/book-reviews-kazuo-ishiguro-klara-and-the-sun/)). **The irony that first targets Klara ends up targeting the reader who laughs at her.**

Matthew Arnold's "Dover Beach" (1867) gives the essay's conclusion a frame. Arnold hears the Sea of Faith's "melancholy, long, withdrawing roar" and finds a world with "neither joy, nor love, nor light". His only answer is human loyalty: "Ah, love, let us be true / To one another!" ([Poetry Foundation](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/43588/dover-beach)). Ishiguro gives back the light Arnold lost, yet his final answer to Capaldi is close to Arnold's. In her closing reflection in Part Six, Klara concludes that what was special about Josie was not inside Josie, where Capaldi searched, but in the people who loved her. Copy the exact sentence from your edition. Katie Fitzpatrick in *The Nation* and the Jewish-thought site 18Forty both read the ending as placing personhood in relationships, not in some core that could be copied ([The Nation](https://www.thenation.com/?p=380342); [18Forty](https://18forty.org/articles/klara-and-the-sun/)). Recent scholarship also supports the novel's refusal to pick a side. Camil Ungureanu and Galyna Maleeva read the novel as a "posthuman humanism", a humanism extended beyond humans ([Res Publica](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/RPUB/article/view/99321)). In a 2026 conference abstract, Ungureanu argues that Ishiguro challenges divides such as human/machine and secular/sacred ([ISFNR 2026 abstract](https://nomadit.co.uk/conference/isfnr2026/paper/92291)). Ishiguro's earlier believers end up disillusioned. In *Never Let Me Go* (2005) the clones' hope of a "deferral" turns out to be a rumour. Miss Emily admits their art was collected "to prove you had souls at all" ([Book Riot](https://bookriot.com/no-one-should-be-asked-to-prove-their-humanity/); [GradeSaver](https://www.gradesaver.com/never-let-me-go/study-guide/summary-chapters-21-23)). In *The Remains of the Day* (1989), Stevens's lifelong devotion to Lord Darlington proves misplaced ([Booker Prizes](https://thebookerprizes.com/the-booker-library/prize-years/1989)). Klara, unlike them, keeps a faith the novel never disproves, which arguably makes her Ishiguro's most sympathetic believer.

Several traps are worth marking in the margin. First, some of the most-quoted "Ishiguro" lines about Klara's god are not his. On *Fresh Air* it was the interviewer, Terry Gross, who said Klara needs the Sun "to stay powered, to stay alive" and "thinks of the sun as God" ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/978138547)). No interview found has Ishiguro calling the Sun "God". Second, the question "is she allowed to hold on to that faith, or would I shatter it for her?" appears as Ishiguro's in one transcript of *A Word on Words* and as the interviewer's in another, so leave it out ([A Word on Words](https://awordonwords.org/klara-and-the-sun-kazuo-ishiguro/); [PBS](https://www.pbs.org/video/klara-and-the-sun-kazuo-ishiguro-a-word-on-words-npt-l5irm9/)). Third, Shulevitz's description of Klara as an "improbable priestess" of "something very like an ancient nature cult" reached the research only second-hand, so paraphrase it. Fourth, credit the "humans believe in science" sentence to *Front Porch Republic* rather than to Griffis by name, because its author is unconfirmed. ## Conclusion

The research changes the shape of the essay question. "Faith versus science" sounds like a contest with a winner, but Ishiguro builds the novel so that each side borrows the other's tools. Klara reaches her faith through observation and inference. Capaldi cannot pitch his science without asking for "faith". The Sun is both Klara's literal power source and her god. So the novel is less interested in whether a scientific age should give up faith than in what a society that can edit genes and copy personalities still has to take on trust. By Part Six its answer is love, with the Sun as love's image. An essay that argues Ishiguro dissolves the opposition, rather than crowning a winner, sits with the most recent scholarship. It avoids the older argument about whether the Sun "works".

The gaps in the criticism are an opening for the student. No critic found analyses the Father's "superstition" sentence, and none treats lifting as a faith of its own, a faith in technology. Tracing the word "superstition" from Rick's dismissal, through the Father's fear for humanity, to Rick's late doubt is therefore an argument the published reviews leave open. In fifty minutes, context earns the most marks when it does two jobs at once. Ishiguro's own "instinct" line establishes both his intention and the ambiguity. One dated scientific event anchors the "technological age". One echo from the long history of sun worship or lost faith lets the conclusion reach beyond the novel.
