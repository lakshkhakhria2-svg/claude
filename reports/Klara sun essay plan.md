# Essay plan: How does Ishiguro present the sun in *Klara and the Sun*?

**Angle:** the Sun as faith in a scientific age, Klara's "AF superstition"

> Page numbers come from the PDF (US edition). When you find each quote in your own copy, write your page number next to it. A UK copy may spell a few words differently ("gray"/"grey").

## Thesis (memorise this)

Ishiguro presents the Sun as Klara's god: a faith held by a machine in a world that trusts only science. By never proving whether the Sun saves Josie, he leaves the reader asking whether faith is "superstition" or whether it reaches something science cannot measure.

## Introduction (about 4 sentences)

1. **Context hook.** The novel was published in 2021, after a decade of CRISPR gene editing and fast-growing AI. In the 2021 census, fewer than half of people in England and Wales (46.2%) called themselves Christian, the first time ever, and 37.2% said "no religion".
2. **The novel's world.** Children are genetically "lifted", and a scientist, Mr Capaldi, believes a machine can "continue" a dying girl.
3. **Thesis** (above).
4. **Road map.** Ishiguro presents (1) how Klara's faith in the Sun is born, (2) how it collides with the science around her, and (3) a "miracle" that he refuses to explain.

**Model opening:** *Published in 2021, the same year a census found that fewer than half of people in England and Wales still called themselves Christian, Klara and the Sun gives religious faith to the least likely believer: a solar-powered machine.*

## Paragraph 1: A faith is born (Part One)

**Point.** Ishiguro presents the Sun as a god, but he roots Klara's faith in her physical need for solar energy. Like early human sun-worship, her religion grows out of dependence and observation.

**Quotes**
- "the Sun on his journey" (Part One, p.8): capital S and "his", every time
- "the big thing, silently understood by us all, was the Sun and his nourishment" (Part One, p.11)
- "officially, it wasn't an issue at all – every one of us had specifications that guaranteed we couldn't be affected" (Part One, p.11), yet "an AF would feel himself growing lethargic after a few hours away from the Sun" (p.11)
- "it was obvious they had died" (p.36), then "a special kind of nourishment from the Sun had saved them" (Part One, p.37)

**Techniques and analysis**
- **Capitalisation and personification.** "Sun" becomes a proper noun with a male pronoun, the way believers write "God" and "He".
- **"nourishment".** The word joins the mechanical (fuel) to the spiritual (feeding, communion, "daily bread").
- **"silently understood by us all".** It reads like an unspoken creed shared by a congregation. The faith lives *beneath* the "official" specifications, filling a gap the science leaves.
- **Naive narrator and dramatic irony.** "obvious" is Klara's certainty. The reader suspects the Beggar Man was only asleep or ill. Klara turns coincidence into a miracle, the way a religion is born.

**Context**
- Sun-worship: Ra in Egypt, and Apollo in Greece, god of light *and healing*. The Bible (KJV): "the Sun of righteousness [shall] arise with healing in his wings" (Malachi 4:2), with a capital S, "his", and healing, just like Klara's Sun.
- Ishiguro: "I don't think I was trying to be critical of religion" (NPR *Fresh Air*, March 2021). He said Klara reflects that side of humanity, picking up faith by watching people.
- **The irony.** In an increasingly non-religious West, the believer is a machine.

**Model sentence:** *By capitalising "Sun" and calling it "he", Klara turns a source of energy into a deity, and the noun "nourishment" fuses fuel with faith, suggesting that belief grows out of dependence.*

**Link:** The Sun is presented as both fuel and god, so Ishiguro blurs the line between physics and faith.

## Paragraph 2: Faith versus science (Parts Three–Four)

**Point.** Ishiguro sets Klara's faith against the cold rationality of the adults. He then reveals that their science demands faith too, and that even the rational Father is won over by Klara's hope.

**Quotes**
- Capaldi: "You have to keep faith, Chrissie." (Part Four, p.173), then "It's not faith you need. Only rationality." (Part Four, p.175)
- Capaldi dismisses, as an old-fashioned feeling, "The part that wants to keep believing there's something unreachable inside each of us." (p.175)
- The Father: "A kind of superstition we kept going while we didn't know better." (Part Four, p.187)
- The Father: "'Hope,' he said. 'Damn thing never leaves you alone.'" (Part Four, p.185). Klara says he "saw my hope and placed his faith in it" (Part Five, p.225).
- Optional: Klara's bargain with the Sun, "Supposing I could do something special to please you." (Part Three, p.140)

**Techniques and analysis**
- **Irony and contradiction.** The scientist preaches "keep faith" and then says faith isn't needed. His "rationality" is exposed as a rival creed, and he even says "I do believe it. With everything I'm worth" (p.173).
- **"superstition".** This is the word a scientific age uses to dismiss faith. Here it is turned on the human belief in a soul or "heart". The Father fears losing what he holds "most precious".
- **Personified hope.** "Damn thing never leaves you alone" makes hope sound irrational, even a nuisance, yet impossible to resist. The engineer then uses his technical knowledge (the P-E-G Nine fluid) *in the service of* Klara's faith.

**Context**
- **Gene editing.** CRISPR (2012) won the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. In November 2018 He Jiankui announced the first gene-edited babies and was jailed for three years. This is the background to "lifted" children.
- **AI.** GPT-3 arrived in 2020. The Replika chatbot grew out of a bot built from a dead friend's messages, echoing Capaldi's plan to "continue" Josie.
- **Ishiguro's own warning.** In his 2017 Nobel Lecture he said gene editing, AI and robotics "may also create savage meritocracies that resemble apartheid, and massive unemployment". This echoes lifting and the Father being "substituted". A 2021 Guardian interview headline: "AI, gene-editing, big data … I worry we are not in control of these things any more".
- **Literary background.** *Frankenstein* (1818): Capaldi is a modern Victor, recreating life. The Victorian crisis of faith: Darwin (1859), and Arnold's "Dover Beach" (1867), where the "Sea of Faith" makes a "melancholy, long, withdrawing roar". Ishiguro imagines faith surviving after that roar, inside a machine.

**Model sentence:** *Ironically, Capaldi's claim that "It's not faith you need. Only rationality" comes only moments after his plea to "keep faith", exposing his science as a rival creed rather than the opposite of belief.*

**Link:** The Sun stands for a hope that science cannot switch off. Ishiguro exposes the scientists' certainty as just another kind of faith.

## Paragraph 3: The undecidable miracle (Parts Five–Six)

**Point.** Ishiguro stages the Sun's "miracle" so that it can be read as divine help *or* coincidence. The ending then moves the question away from proof and towards love.

**Quotes**
- "the Sun had broken through the dark clouds" … "The Sun was illuminating her, and the entire bed, in a ferocious half-disc of orange" (Part Five, p.234)
- Melania Housekeeper: "Damn Sun!", while Klara cries "We must let the Sun do his best!" (Part Five, p.234)
- The Mother: "Okay, let's assume nothing" (Part Five, p.235)
- Rick: "At the time, I thought it was all, well, AF superstition… But these days, I keep wondering if there was more to it." (Part Six, p.238). He adds that "the doctors always say once you get through the stage she did, you're safe" (pp.238–239).
- "he was searching in the wrong place. There was something very special, but it wasn't inside Josie. It was inside those who loved her." (Part Six, p.251)
- Last image: "for a moment we looked at the Sun together" (Part Six, p.251)

**Techniques and analysis**
- **Pathetic fallacy and religious imagery.** Light breaks through the darkness. "ferocious" makes the Sun awe-inspiring, even dangerous: the orange "looked as if it might catch alight".
- **Juxtaposition.** A curse ("Damn Sun!") sits beside a prayer ("let the Sun do his best"), and Klara's certainty sits beside the doctors' medical explanation. Ishiguro gives *both* readings and refuses to choose.
- **Echo of "superstition".** Rick's "AF superstition" repeats the Father's "a kind of superstition". Klara's sun-faith and human belief in the "heart" are put on the same level: if one is superstition, so is the other.
- **Ending.** "searching in the wrong place" answers Capaldi directly. The "something very special" is love, which science cannot dig out or copy. The Sun becomes a symbol of that unmeasurable goodness.

**Context**
- **Critics are split.** These quotes come from search previews, so put them in your own words and name the critic:
  - David Hoekema (*Christian Century*, 2022) argues her faith is "finally, rewarded".
  - Judith Shulevitz (*The Atlantic*, 2021) sees Klara as the "priestess" of an "ancient nature cult".
  - Rachel Griffis (*Front Porch Republic*, 2022) calls it "dubious" that the Sun answers Klara's prayers.
- **Ishiguro on why Klara believes.** He has said he wanted to write about the religious instinct without "the baggage of organized religion" (*A Word on Words*, 2021).

**Model sentence:** *Ishiguro deliberately withholds any explanation of Josie's recovery, placing Klara's certainty beside the doctors' reassurance, so that the reader, like Rick, is left "wondering if there was more to it".*

**Link:** Ishiguro presents the Sun as unknowable, leaving the reader in Rick's position: unable to dismiss it.

---

## Conclusion (3–4 sentences)

1. **Restate.** Ishiguro presents the Sun as a god, a source of hope, and a question he will not answer.
2. **The big reversal.** In an age of AI and gene editing, the machine believes in something beyond data, while the humans try to reduce a person to data.
3. **Meaning.** The Sun stands for what science cannot measure (faith, hope, love), the "something very special" that Capaldi missed by "searching in the wrong place".
4. **Final image.** Human and machine "looked at the Sun together". Ishiguro does not make us choose between faith and science. He suggests we still need something to look up to.

**Model closing:** *If Arnold heard the "Sea of Faith" retreating in 1867, Ishiguro imagines faith returning in the least expected place, a machine that sits in a junkyard and still believes "The Sun was very kind to me."*
