## 1. What has Ishiguro said about Klara's belief in the Sun: does he call it religion or faith, and does he endorse it, critique it, or leave it open?

### Takeaway
He calls Klara's relationship with the Sun "kind of a religious thing". He describes it as a human "instinct" that he wanted to write about "without any of the baggage of organized religion". He told NPR's *Fresh Air* (Terry Gross, first broadcast 17 March 2021, not *All Things Considered*) that he wasn't "trying to be critical of religion": Klara "reflect[s] that aspect of humanity". He neither mocks her faith nor confirms it. He presents it as something Klara learned by watching people, and by one transcript's account he deliberately chose not to "shatter" it.

### Inferences
- Ishiguro takes a neutral-to-sympathetic stance. He was "not … critical of religion", and he calls Klara's devotion an "instinct" and an "aspect of humanity". On his account, her Sun-worship is something she learned by watching people, so the most technological character in the book carries one of the oldest human habits: faith, prayer, bargaining and hope. This supports the thesis that the Sun stands for faith that survives in a scientific and technological age.
- His phrase "without any of the baggage of organized religion" helps explain why the novel never mentions God, a church or scripture. The Sun is faith reduced to its instinct, cut loose from doctrine.
- 1-D (if it is his line) and the critics' readings point the same way: he left the question open on purpose. The novel does not confirm or disprove that the Sun helped Josie. An essay can describe this as Ishiguro refusing to let science have the last word, without claiming that he endorses the belief.
- If 1-B is accurate, his own Japanese background treated religion as a casual, cultural matter rather than doctrine. That fits his "instinct" framing. This is a context point, not a central quote.

### Inferences
- "I worry we are not in control" and "staggering" benefits together show ambivalence, not technophobia. An essay can argue that Ishiguro is less worried about the technology itself than about people failing to think through what it does to love and to the individual.
- Set against this, the Sun is an ancient power that nobody controls. Klara puts her faith in it inside a society that assumes it can engineer everything, from lifting children to building an AF to "continue" Josie. That contrast is the core of the "faith in a scientific age" argument.
- "Lifted" is a euphemism Ishiguro chose for gene editing. It has a near-religious ring ("raised up"), which an essay could set beside Klara's language for the Sun. This is an interpretation, not something Ishiguro is reported as saying.

## 4. What has he said about the children's-story origin (his daughter Naomi's reaction) and about why the Sun matters to Klara (she is solar-powered)?

### Takeaway
The book began as an idea for a picture book for young children, with Klara as a doll-like figure or a small animal. Ishiguro recalls his daughter Naomi, herself a writer, telling him: "there is no way you can go anywhere near a young child with that story – you'll traumatize them". So he turned the idea into a novel. On the Sun, he explains Klara's faith as following from her being solar-powered: "she thinks everything good comes from the sun. That's where she gets her nourishment." She then assumes that the humans she sees from the shop window are "in the same position".

- The children's-story origin helps explain the fairy-tale logic of Klara's faith: the bargain with the Sun, the "special nourishment", the Sun as a person. It is a child's way of believing, kept inside an adult novel about AI and gene editing. Ishiguro's own descriptions of Klara as a "tabula rasa", "childlike and very open" (1-E), support this.
- His "nourishment" explanation roots her faith in her physical nature, solar power, and in a generalisation from her own case to humans ("in the same position"). Her religion is reached by reasoning from what she observes, as a scientist might, using limited evidence (the Beggar Man and his dog "revived" by the Sun). An essay can turn this into a strong point: in a scientific age, Klara's faith is built with the method of science, from observation and inference, yet it ends in prayer.
- The Naomi story is also useful for context. A young reader's judgement that the tale was too disturbing for children points to the darkness under the novel's gentle, sunlit surface.
